Sharing with a family: groups inside one vault

Drawn on 23 Nov 2026 for docs/format.md (“Vaults and groups”, “Membership and revocation”) and revised the same day after the owner asked that sharing apply to items and groups inside one vault instead of separate vaults. Approved by the owner on 2026-11-23. Not built. The family is Alex (the person using the mocks, alex@example.net), Jordan, and two children, Riley and Sam. Inside the frames a person sees groups, people, roles and passwords; the technical words are explained in this page's prose and in the decisions at the end. In frames, “vault” always means the person's whole vault, never a shared unit. All data is fictional.

What a first-time person must know here.
  • Which thing? A group. A person has one vault, shown as one list. Inside it are groups: Only Me, which is private and always there, and shared groups such as Family and Sam's logins. An item is in exactly one group. Sharing an item means moving it into a group.
  • Who can see an item? Everyone in its group, and nobody else. Each item's page says which group it is in and who that is, in one line. Groups are flat: folders or tags inside a group never change who can see an item.
  • Who holds the key? Each person, through their own Apple Account on their own devices. There is no family password and no server that can open a group. People who manage members can add and remove people.
  • What is true when an item moves? It shows to everyone in the new group from now on. Its previous passwords stay behind in the group it left. Moving it back out does not take back what people already saw. New logins saved from Safari go to Only Me unless the person chose another default.
  • How do people join? A new person is invited once, in person, with a code and a short set of words. After that, adding them to another group is a pick from people already known, with no code.
  • Roles, in plain words. “Manages members” (can also change items), “Can change items”, “Can view only”. View only does not stop a person from seeing or copying what they can see, and the screens say so.
  • What does removing someone do? They get nothing new in that group. What they already saw cannot be taken back, so Psss lists the passwords they could see and lets the person mark each one changed.

1. Groups in the list

A person's vault is every group they belong to, shown as one list. Search, AutoFill and Recently Deleted span all of them. Only Me always exists and is private.

iPad: uses the Mac sidebar when the window is wide, with the Groups section and the member counts, and the iPhone list when it is narrow. Items in the iPad list show a small group name only when more than one group is on screen at once.

2. An item and its group

An item is in exactly one group. Who can see it has one short answer: the people in that group.

iPad: shows the same page in the detail pane of a wide window and pushed from the list on a narrow one. Move to Group… and Share… are buttons in the pane, not in the toolbar.

3. Moving one item, and sharing it with people

Two ways in to the same thing. Move to Group… starts from groups. Share… starts from people and finds or makes the group that has exactly them. Nothing is copied, so there is never a second version of an item to keep in step.

iPad: shows the two sheets as popovers anchored to the buttons in a wide window, and as sheets in a narrow one. The confirmation is the same sheet.

4. Creating a group, and adding a person who is already known

A person is invited once. Their key then exists in the vault, so adding them to a second group hands them that group's key and nothing more. That is why no code is needed again.

iPad: uses the same two sheets. On a wide window they are popovers from the Groups section and from Add Someone….

5. Moving several items at once

The case the owner described: select, Move to Group…, done. One confirmation for the whole selection, not one per item.

iPad: uses the iPhone selection list in a narrow window and the Mac list with its toolbar in a wide one. The confirmation is a popover from the Move to Group… button.

6. Who is in a group

A person's role is what they can do, not who they are. Each member has their own key, so adding or removing a person never means sharing or changing a password.

iPad: follows the Mac layout in a wide window, with the members in the detail pane next to the group list, and the iPhone layout in a narrow one. Add Someone… opens a form sheet.

7. Inviting someone new, in person

This happens once per person. The code carries a one-time secret, so iCloud or a server cannot put its own key between two people. The words are a short fingerprint of both devices' keys. If they match on both screens, nobody is in the middle. Both people see the same screens; the platforms in a pair are chosen only so that iPhone and Mac both appear, and the two people may use any mix of devices.

iPad: uses the iPhone layout in a narrow window and the Mac layout in a wide one. A form sheet on iPad has the same buttons at the bottom. The camera uses the system scanner.

8. A child

A child's own logins are in a group shared with their parents. The child is also a view-only member of Family. View only is enforced by the devices, not by storage: a change signed by a view-only person is ignored by every Psss device, so another app cannot get around it. It does not hide anything. A person who can see a password can copy it, and the screens say so. It protects Family from accidents, not from the reader.

iPad: follows the Mac layout in a wide window and the iPhone layout in a narrow one. On a child's iPad the detail pane has no Edit button, and the Edit menu item is absent too.

9. Removing a person from a group

Removing a person starts a new key for that group, given to everyone who is left. New changes use it. The removed person's devices cannot read anything added after that. What they already read, they keep in their memory and in anything they copied; no design can take that back. So the second screen is the useful one. It is scoped to the group: the person stays in their other groups.

iPad: shows the Mac alert as a popover from the Remove button in a wide window and the iPhone action sheet in a narrow one. The list is the same sheet.

10. Errors and edge states

A failed invitation shares nothing. Every error says so, names the person, and says how to start again.

iPad: uses the same screens. A person on an iPad without iCloud sees the iPhone wording with “iPad”.

Decisions for the owner

  1. NEW. Sharing is by groups inside one person's vault. There is no separate Family vault, and an item is in exactly one group.
    Rejected: Separate vaults for family, partner and children (the previous design); an item in several groups; copies of an item in other people's vaults.
    Why: The owner asked for it, and it is how Apple Passwords does it. A person with one list and a few groups has nothing to learn but the groups. One group per item keeps “who can see this?” to one short answer. Anything that must be in two groups can be two items.
  2. NEW. Share… is a shortcut that moves the item into the group with exactly the chosen people, making one if none exists, after a confirmation that names the group and the people.
    Rejected: Share… making a copy; Share… adding people to the item's current group; no confirmation.
    Why: A person who thinks in people gets what they expect. Adding people to the current group would show every other item in it, which is the surprise to prevent. The confirmation is where that is visible. Risk: many one-off groups named for pairs. The names can be changed, and the list says how many people are in each.
  3. NEW. Moving an item into a shared group states three truths on the sheet: it shows to everyone in the group from now on, previous passwords stay behind, moving it back does not take back what was seen. New logins from Safari go to Only Me unless the person chose another default.
    Rejected: Saying nothing; a confirmation only for the first move; asking which group on every save.
    Why: The rules are true and surprising: a previous password is a secret too, and the damage of a wrong move cannot be undone. The Safari default keeps the “one tap on Save” bar and makes sharing a deliberate act.
  4. NEW. Groups are flat. Folders or tags inside a group never change who can see an item.
    Rejected: Nested groups with inherited access, such as Family containing Kids; per-item visibility settings.
    Why: Who can see an item must have one short answer. Inheritance makes it a calculation (the group, its parents, exceptions), and a mistake there exposes a password. Two flat groups with overlapping people give the same result with a plain answer.
  5. NEW. A person is invited once, in person, with the code and words. Adding a known person to another group is a pick, with no code.
    Rejected: A code for every group; remote invitations by link.
    Why: The code and words exist to keep storage and messaging out of the middle, and that is a property of the person's key, which exists after the first invitation. Pushback: “in person” excludes a grandparent in another country; a video call works for the words. I would allow that in the first version and not build a remote invitation.
  6. NEW. A child's own logins live in a group shared with the parents (Sam's logins), and the child is a view-only member of Family.
    Rejected: A separate vault for each child; the child's logins in Family; no parent access.
    Why: Parents can help with a lost password because they are in the group, and the child cannot change Family. Pushback: view only is not a parental control. The child can still see and copy every password in Family and cannot save a school login into Family. A group that the child can change, shared with the parents, is where those go.
  7. NEW. No recovery pieces for a group.
    Rejected: Two of three pieces held by managers and a printed card.
    Why: Each person's key comes back through their own Apple Account, and another manager can let a member back in. A group with one manager who loses their account is the remaining risk, and the answer is a second manager.
  8. Role names are “Manages members”, “Can change items” and “Can view only”. Managing members also includes changing items.
    Rejected: Owner, Editor and Viewer; a toggle for each right.
    Why: The names say what a person can do. “Manages members” needs the footer sentence about also changing items. If the owner prefers, show two lines per person.
  9. On the words screen neither button is primary.
    Rejected: The Words Match as the filled button.
    Why: A filled “match” button invites tapping without looking. The cost is that the screen breaks the one primary action rule.
  10. Removing a person from a group comes first and the list of passwords second. The list is scoped to the group and marks a password changed when a new one is saved here, with a manual Mark as Changed.
    Rejected: Blocking removal until the passwords are changed; an automatic password change; removing the person from every group at once.
    Why: The owner may need to remove someone at once, and changing 29 passwords takes days. The list persists so the work can be done over time. The person stays in their other groups. The manual button is a claim and Psss cannot verify it.
  11. A view-only member is not offered Save a Backup Copy or Export as KeePass File.
    Rejected: Offering both to every member.
    Why: It keeps a child from writing everything in Family into a file that stays in Downloads. It does not stop a determined person from copying passwords one by one, and the screens do not claim it does.
  12. The person-facing word is “group”. “Vault” in a frame means the person's whole vault.
    Rejected: “Shared vault”, “folder”, “bucket”, “space”, “circle”.
    Why: “Folder” suggests tidying, which would hide that a group decides who sees an item. “Group” matches Apple Passwords' shared groups, which the owner pointed to.
  13. Question: On iPhone, are groups a list reached from a pill, or the pills themselves?
    Drawn as: A Groups pill that opens a list with counts. The pills of the search-first screen cannot carry a member count without being cut off.
    To verify: whether the owner wants the iPhone to keep no folder list. A list is a departure from the iPhone design recorded in docs/experience.md.
  14. Question: Can an item be in two groups?
    Drawn as: No: one group, one answer to who sees it.
    To verify: with the owner. A shared login that also belongs in Work is a duplicate item in two groups.
  15. Question: Can a child's Apple Account accept a shared group?
    Drawn as: Sam joins like anyone else.
    To verify: whether a CloudKit share can be accepted by a child account, the open spike in docs/format.md. If not, a child joins through a folder or server, or not at all.
  16. Question: Can a person who manages members remove another person who manages members?
    Drawn as: Yes: Alex and Jordan can each remove the other. The screens do not show a rule against it.
    To verify: what the format's chain of changes allows. If it can, a falling-out costs the other their access. If not, only the person who invited them can. Both need to be said on the removal screen.
  17. Question: Where is the list of passwords to change stored, and who sees it?
    Drawn as: In the group, for everyone who manages members, synced.
    To verify: If it is only on the device that removed the person, the other manager does not see it. Storing it in the group makes it data other members can read; it shows who was removed and when.
  18. Question: Which passwords are on the list?
    Drawn as: The 29 current logins in Family at the time of removal.
    To verify: whether items in Recently Deleted and previous passwords of those items count (the removed person could read them). Probably they should.
  19. Question: What happens to a person's items when they are removed from a group they put items into?
    Drawn as: Not drawn. Items stay in the group.
    To verify: whether items added by the removed person can be moved back to them, and whether Sam, removed from Sam's logins, takes anything.

Conflicts with Apple's guidance

  1. Joining is by QR code and words, not by AirDrop or the share sheet.
    Apple Passwords shares through Messages and AirDrop, which are quicker. The code and words are slower on purpose, to keep storage and messaging out of the middle. The cost is a flow that feels more like pairing a device than sharing a file. It happens once per person.
  2. The words screen has two equal buttons and no primary action.
    The HIG asks for one default button on a screen. A default on “The Words Match” is the unsafe one to make habitual, so none is chosen. The cost is a screen that follows no standard pattern.
  3. Role names are written by ability, not borrowed from Apple's Family Sharing (organizer, parent, child).
    Apple's family roles are tied to an Apple Family and an age. Psss has no knowledge of either, and “child” would mislead about what the role does. The cost is a second vocabulary beside Apple's.
  4. Groups are a list on iPhone reached from a pill, and a sidebar section on Mac and iPad.
    The HIG uses a sidebar or tab bar for top-level areas on iPhone. The iPhone design here is search-first with filter pills, recorded earlier as a departure; a Groups pill continues it.
  5. Move to Group… and Share… are labeled buttons on the item's page, not a menu or toolbar icon.
    Apple's detail pages put such actions in a toolbar menu. The project rule is visible, labeled actions. The cost is more buttons on the page.
  6. Selection summary and Move to Group… are separate toolbar items on Mac.
    This follows the project's toolbar rule and the HIG: passive status apart from actions. The iPhone uses the navigation title and a bottom button.
  7. A camera view drawn in the frame.
    The HIG says to use the system scanner. It is drawn as a placeholder only; the build should use the system data scanner.
  8. Several buttons, one per row, in the password list (Mark as Changed).
    The HIG discourages controls in list rows. Each row has one labeled button and no row action, so the control is not competing with a tap. An icon-only tick would hide the action.
  9. A red Remove button on the person's page, in a stack with other actions.
    Apple uses a destructive row at the end of a form. Kept as a labeled button because the project asks for visible, labeled actions. Red is used only because the action removes a person.
