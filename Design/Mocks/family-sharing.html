Sharing a vault with a family

Drawn on 23 Nov 2026 for the family model in docs/vaults.md and the membership rules in docs/format.md. Nothing here is built. The family is Alex (the person using the mocks, alex@example.net), Jordan, and two children, Riley and Sam. Inside the frames a person sees people, roles and passwords; the technical words are explained in this page's prose and in the decisions at the end. All data is fictional.

What a first-time person must know here.
  • Which thing? A vault called Family, shared by four people. Each person also keeps their own vaults, which Family does not touch. Sharing is per vault, never per item.
  • Who holds the key? Each person, on their own devices. There is no family password and no server that can open it. Anyone who manages members can invite and remove people.
  • Roles, in plain words. “Manages members” (can also change items), “Can change items”, “Can view only”. View only stops a person from changing Family; it does not stop them from seeing or copying what they can see, and the screens say so.
  • How do people join? In person, with a code the inviter shows and the other person scans, and a short set of words both confirm. The code is what stops iCloud or any other storage from putting its own key in between.
  • What does removing someone do? They get nothing new. What they already saw cannot be taken back, so Psss lists the passwords they could see and lets the person mark each one changed.
  • What if everyone loses their devices? Two of three pieces open Family again: one for each adult and a printed card. Psss cannot recover it with fewer.
  • Where does Family live? In iCloud, shared with the others, and on each person's device. For a person without iCloud, in a folder or on a server (frames 29 to 32).

1. Who is in Family

A person's role is what they can do, not who they are. Each member has their own key, wrapped for them alone, so adding or removing a person never means sharing or changing a password.

iPad: follows the Mac layout in a wide window, with the members in the detail pane next to the vault list, and the iPhone layout in a narrow one. Invite Someone… on iPad opens a form sheet. Roles and devices are the same.

2. Inviting someone in person

The code carries a one-time secret, so iCloud or a server cannot put its own key between two people. The words are a short fingerprint of both devices' keys. If they match on both screens, nobody is in the middle. Both people see the same screens; the platforms in a pair are chosen only so that iPhone and Mac both appear, and the two people may use any mix of devices.

iPad: uses the iPhone layout in a narrow window and the Mac layout in a wide one. A form sheet on iPad has the same buttons at the bottom. The camera uses the system scanner.

3. A child with view-only access

View only is enforced by the devices, not by storage: a change signed by a view-only person is ignored by every Psss device, so another app cannot get around it. It does not hide anything. A person who can see a password can copy it, take a photo of it and tell it to someone, and the screens say so. It protects Family from accidents, not from the reader.

iPad: follows the Mac layout in a wide window and the iPhone layout in a narrow one. For a child's iPad the detail pane has no Edit button; the Edit menu item is absent too.

4. Removing a member

Removing a person starts a new key for Family, given to everyone who is left. New changes use it. The removed person's devices cannot read anything added after that. What they already read, they keep in their memory and in anything they copied; no design can take that back. So the second screen is the useful one.

iPad: shows the Mac alert as a popover from the Remove button on a wide window and the iPhone action sheet in a narrow one. The list is the same sheet.

5. Recovery for Family

Family should not depend on one person remembering a secret, and should not be openable by one person alone. The key is split into three pieces and any two put it back together: one piece for each adult and one on a printed card. This needs an audited implementation or published test vectors before it is built.

iPad: uses the Mac layout in a wide window. Printing from iPad uses the system print sheet, and the card is scanned with the system scanner.

6. Errors and edge states

A failed invitation shares nothing. Every error says so, names the person, and says how to start again.

iPad: uses the same screens. A person on an iPad without iCloud sees the iPhone wording with “iPad”.

Decisions for the owner

  1. The three roles are “Manages members”, “Can change items” and “Can view only”. Managing members also includes changing items.
    Rejected: Owner, Editor and Viewer (borrowed from file sharing, and an “owner” of a family vault would be read as the one who owns the passwords); a toggle for each right.
    Why: The names say what a person can do. “Manages members” is the one name that needs the footer sentence about also changing items; a person might assume it only manages people. If the owner would rather, show two lines per person (“Manages members”, “Can change items”).
  2. Children are given “Can view only” by the inviter when the inviter chooses it. Psss does not know who is a child and has no “child” role.
    Rejected: A child role chosen by age; reading the Apple Family; making view only the default.
    Why: Psss has no accounts and cannot know ages. A role is a choice about what a person may do. Pushback: view only is not a parental control. The child can still see and copy every password, and cannot save a new login into Family (a school login would go to the child's own vault). If the owner wants children to add logins, give them Can change items. The frames say this, but the owner should know that “view only for children” protects Family from accidents, not from the child.
  3. People join only in person (or on a call you trust), with a code and three words. No invitation link.
    Rejected: A link sent in Messages or Mail; a code typed from a message; digits instead of words.
    Why: A link would pass through a service that can swap the key, which is the attack the code prevents. Three words are easier to say aloud and to compare than 6 digits or a hex fingerprint. Pushback: “in person” excludes a grandparent in another country. A video call works for the words, as the code can be read out. I would allow that in the first version and not build a remote invitation.
  4. On the words screen neither button is primary.
    Rejected: The Words Match as the filled button.
    Why: A filled “match” button invites tapping without looking. The cost is that the screen breaks the one primary action rule.
  5. Removing a person comes first and the list of passwords second. The list marks a password changed when a new one is saved here, and has a manual Mark as Changed.
    Rejected: Blocking removal until the passwords are changed; an automatic password change; a list the removed person never leaves.
    Why: The owner may need to remove someone at once, for example a lost phone or a falling out, and changing 42 passwords takes days. The list persists so the work can be done over time. The manual button is a person's claim and Psss cannot verify it.
  6. The removal confirmation and the list both say what cannot be taken back.
    Rejected: Saying the person “loses access”, which suggests everything is gone.
    Why: They lose access to anything new. They keep what they saw. Saying less would be false.
  7. A Family vault suggests a recovery card after the first person is invited, not before and not required.
    Rejected: Making recovery a step of creating Family; leaving it to Setup alone.
    Why: Before the first invitation there is only one person and nothing to recover. After it, the prompt is the right moment. Requiring it would delay sharing.
  8. The inviter chooses how Family reaches a person: iCloud, a folder or a server.
    Rejected: The invited person is asked on their device.
    Why: The inviter knows the family's setup. A folder or server needs the code to carry what the other device needs, which the inviter's device holds.
  9. A view-only member is not offered Save a Backup Copy or Export as KeePass File for Family.
    Rejected: Offering both to every member.
    Why: It keeps a child from writing the whole of Family into a file that stays in Downloads. It does not stop a determined person from copying passwords one by one, and the screens do not claim it does.
  10. The words “recovery card” for Family and “recovery kit” for a person.
    Rejected: One word for both; “recovery kit” for the printed Family card.
    Why: They are different things with different rules (two of three, against one sheet). Two words stop a person from thinking one printed sheet is enough for Family. The risk is that two similar words are confused; the owner may prefer one.
  11. Question: Can a child's Apple Account accept a shared vault?
    Drawn as: Sam, a child, joins like anyone else.
    To verify: whether a CloudKit share can be accepted by a child account, which is the open spike in docs/format.md. If not, a child joins through a folder or server (frames 29 to 31), or is not a member of Family at all.
  12. Question: Can a person who manages members remove another person who manages members?
    Drawn as: Yes, either of Alex and Jordan can remove the other. The screens do not show a rule against it.
    To verify: what the format's chain of changes allows. If one manager can remove another, a falling-out costs the other their access. If not, only the person who invited them can. Both need to be said on the removal screen.
  13. Question: Where does each adult's recovery piece live, and does a recovery kit bring it back?
    Drawn as: Frames 25 and 26 say Alex's piece was found with the recovery kit, as if it is stored with Alex's own keys.
    To verify: with docs/vaults.md (Shamir pieces, two of three) and the key layers in docs/format.md. If the piece is only on the device, losing all devices loses the piece and a second adult must be asked, so the frame would change.
  14. Question: Where is the list of passwords to change stored, and who sees it?
    Drawn as: In Family, for everyone who manages members, and synced.
    To verify: If it is only on the device that removed the person, the other manager does not see it. Storing it in the vault is possible, but it is then data other members can read and shows who was removed and when.
  15. Question: Which passwords are on the list?
    Drawn as: All 42 passwords in Family at the time of removal.
    To verify: whether items in Recently Deleted and previous passwords count (the removed person could read those). Frames 21 and 22 count only current logins, which is probably too few.
  16. Question: Can a person join from far away, by reading the words out on a call?
    Drawn as: Not drawn. Only in-person flows are shown.
    To verify: the security of a typed code sent through a messaging app followed by words read aloud. The words step already defeats a swapped key, so this could be allowed.

Conflicts with Apple's guidance

  1. Joining is by QR code and words, not by AirDrop or the share sheet.
    Apple Passwords shares through Messages and AirDrop, which are quicker. The code and words are slower on purpose, to keep storage and messaging out of the middle. The cost is a flow that feels more like pairing a device than sharing a file.
  2. The words screen has two equal buttons and no primary action.
    The HIG asks for one default button on a screen. A default on “The Words Match” is the unsafe one to make habitual, so none is chosen. The cost is a screen that follows no standard pattern.
  3. Role names are written by ability, not borrowed from Apple's Family Sharing (organizer, parent, child).
    Apple's family roles are tied to an Apple Family and an age. Psss has no knowledge of either, and “child” would mislead about what the role does. The cost is a second vocabulary beside Apple's.
  4. A camera view drawn in the frame.
    The HIG says to use the system scanner. It is drawn as a placeholder only; the build should use the system data scanner.
  5. Several buttons, one per row, in the password list (Mark as Changed).
    The HIG discourages controls in list rows. Each row has one labeled button and no row action, so the control is not competing with a tap. An icon-only tick would hide the action.
  6. A red Remove button on the person's page, in a stack with other actions.
    Apple uses a destructive row at the end of a form. Kept as a labeled button because the project asks for visible, labeled actions. Red is used only because the action removes a person.
