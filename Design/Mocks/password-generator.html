Password generator

Mock for approval, drawn on 23 Nov 2026, revised the same day after the owner’s first review. Awaiting approval. Nothing here is built, and no document has been changed to match. The plan behind it is docs/plans/password-generator.md. This page was written without a browser and was checked by reading the markup back, not by viewing it rendered. Check it in a browser, in light and dark, before approving.

Changes after the owner’s first review, 2026-11-23.
  • The default is what Apple generates. Owner decision. Twenty characters in three groups of six, joined by two hyphens, with one digit and one capital. Words are now a type the person can choose, not the default. The page no longer offers a choice of default.
  • The password is an object and is hidden until Show. Its own monospaced field, wrapping at the hyphens, with Show, Copy and New always visible. The strength sentence moved: none at the default, and one line beside the control that caused it when a choice makes the password weaker than the default. There is no longer an exception to the rule that a secret is never shown without a gesture.
  • Controls. None before Customize. Inside it: a Type list, a slider for Length, and segmented controls for the small counts. A frame shows the rejected alternative, sliders for the counts.
What a first-time person must know here.
  • What is being made? A new password for one site. It is hidden until they press Show, and can be copied at any time.
  • Where does it live? Only in the vault on this device once they press Use This Password. Nothing is uploaded and an unused password is never stored. The sheet says so in one line.
  • Who holds the key? Face ID opens the vault to save the login. Showing the suggestion needs no unlock, because it reads nothing from the vault.
  • How do they get out? Cancel, always visible. Nothing is saved or filled. If the site says no, one button, The Site Didn’t Accept It, and one answer make a password that fits.
  • What happens next? The button names it: Use This Password fills the site’s fields and saves the login.

Every password on this page is invented. The shape follows Apple’s published description: consonant-vowel-consonant syllables, three groups of six, two hyphens, one capital, one digit next to a hyphen or at the end. Apple does not publish its 19 consonants or its capital positions, so Psss’s version is a reconstruction and the copy says “like Safari makes”, not “the same as Apple’s”. Digits and symbols in a password are drawn bold and hyphens in the secondary colour, so they stand out without colour.

1. In the app: a new login, and changing a password

One control, with the password in its own field. At the default there are no controls and no strength sentence: Apple-style needs no explanation.

2. Customize

Customize is a plain button under Use This Password. It reveals a Type list and then the controls for the chosen type. Length is a slider because the range is wide and the exact value rarely matters; the counts are segmented because there are four values and the person should see them all and tap one.

iPad: regular width uses the Mac sheet, compact width the iPhone form. At the largest Dynamic Type size the password box wraps at a hyphen onto more lines and the three small buttons stack; segments keep single digits, so they stay on one line. The masked box has the same group shape as the password, so its length and structure are visible and its characters are not.

3. A site asks for a new password (iPhone and iPad, iOS 26.2 or later)

Mac has no system request for this. Its frames show the bundled Safari extension’s popup, which uses the same card. In the sheet the person never needs to read the password: Use This Password fills and saves. It is hidden, with Show and Copy one tap away. Taps in the normal case: focus the password field, tap Use This Password, one Face ID glance. What the system and our code do today, and the table of save events, are in the plan (section 3b).

iPad: the sheet is the iPhone sheet at medium height, a form sheet at regular width. If the system calls us without UI instead, it shows its own list of up to three passwords from us, in priority order starting with the person’s default (Apple-style is returned as the system’s strong kind, Words as passphrase, letters and digits as alphanumeric), each satisfying the rules. After the person picks one, the filled-event save writes the login with no question. I could not verify the system’s own list on a device.

4. No generate request: below iOS 26.2, some apps, and everything on Mac outside Safari

Psss cannot see the page, so it cannot fill it. What we honestly offer is the editor’s card with a labeled Copy Password. The login is stored as a draft first, so leaving to paste does not lose it. There is no save-after-submit prompt on Mac (no API), and the caption says so once instead of apologising.

A site’s rules are remembered with its login in both options, as a hidden field, so “change password” works first time on every device. The other two open questions, when the login is saved and the recovery panel, are in the decisions below (11 and 12).

Decisions for the owner

  1. The default is Apple’s shape: 20 characters, three groups of six joined by two hyphens, consonant-vowel-consonant syllables, one digit next to a hyphen or at the end, one capital. About 71 to 72 bits. Decided by the owner, 2026-11-23.
    Rejected: Words only (15% letters-and-digit compatibility against 48%); six words and one number, my earlier recommendation (83 bits and equally compatible, but 48 characters long and unfamiliar); Apple-style plus a symbol or extra digits (not the shape sites know).
    Why: The owner chose it for familiarity and site acceptance: it is the password Safari makes for billions of people, so sites accept it, and anyone who has used Apple Passwords recognises it. Costs the plan states: it is not the strongest option (71 bits against 83 for six words and 115 for 20 random characters), our version is a reconstruction because Apple does not publish its 19 consonants, its capital positions or its offensive-word list, and 36% of the awkward sites in Apple’s own list cap length under 18 and get random characters instead.
  2. Nothing is visible before Customize. Customize shows a Type list (Apple-style, Random characters, Words, each with one sentence), then the controls for that type. Apple-style has none.
    Rejected: Showing Words, Numbers and Symbols counts on the card (the first draft; decisions the family does not need); a Type switch without sentences.
    Why: The default just works. Each type is described where it is chosen, so no one has to guess what Words means.
  3. The password is its own field: monospaced at the title size, spaced, grouped, wrapping only at a hyphen. Digits and symbols are bold, hyphens are secondary.
    Rejected: Plain text in the card’s paragraph (the first draft read as gibberish at the top of a paragraph); colouring digits and symbols as some managers do (colour alone fails for some people and red is reserved for deletion).
    Why: It reads as an object to be copied or typed, not as prose. Weight and tone survive colour blindness and monochrome. A monospaced face at the title size keeps to two sizes.
  4. The password is hidden as dots until Show, in the editor and the AutoFill sheet. Show, Hide, Copy and New are always visible labeled buttons. In Setup the preview is an example and is shown.
    Rejected: The first group visible (it exposes a third of the password to a shoulder surfer or a photo); showing it by default with an exception to the secrets rule (the first draft); hiding Copy until Show.
    Why: The rule that a secret is never shown without a gesture now holds with no exception, the gesture being Show. In the sheet nobody needs to read it. Copy works while hidden and follows the clipboard rule: local only, 60 seconds. VoiceOver never reads the masked box. In Setup nothing is stored, so there is nothing to hide.
  5. No strength sentence at the default. A line appears only when the person’s choice is weaker than the default (under 71 bits), directly under the control that caused it, in plain words with a time at a trillion guesses a second and what to change.
    Rejected: A sentence under the password (it read like part of the password); a line in the section footer or caption (away from its cause); a coloured bar or a percentage.
    Why: Apple-style needs no explanation, and the family should not read a number every time. A hint tied to the control tells the person what to change.
  6. Length is a slider (12 to 64, the number shown). Words (4 to 7), Numbers (0 to 3) and Symbols (0 to 3) are segmented controls. Type is a list of rows with a check.
    Rejected: Sliders for the counts (frame 7: four values on a track hide the other values and need a readout); steppers (the first draft: three taps to go from 0 to 3, and nothing shows the other values); a stepper or text field for Length (52 taps, or a keyboard for a number).
    Why: A slider is for a wide range where the relative value matters; a segmented control is for a few exclusive choices, all visible, one tap, one VoiceOver announcement. The HIG points are from memory, because the pages could not be loaded. VoiceOver’s slider step must be verified as one character, not ten per cent.
  7. The site’s rules are met silently. One plain line appears only when the kind had to change (random characters because the cap is under 18) or words were reduced. Hyphens and separators are adjusted without comment.
    Rejected: Always showing the rules; asking the person which rule to relax.
    Why: People do not know Apple’s rule language and cannot decide anything useful. A password that looks different from the usual one would be a surprise, so that case names its cause.
  8. Changes are remembered as the new default at once. Reset to Recommended appears only when they differ.
    Rejected: A “Use as default” switch (a second decision on every use); a Save button on the card.
    Why: One memory and one way back. A change a site forced is never remembered as a preference.
  9. The defaults are per device, in the shared App Group defaults, read by the app and the extensions (section 6 compares the alternative). Still open.
    Rejected: Storing them in the vault: it needs Face ID before the AutoFill sheet can show a suggestion and a change to the vault format for a setting few people change.
    Why: The sheet must appear without an unlock, because generating reads nothing from the vault. With Apple-style as the default almost nobody will customise.
  10. A site’s rules are remembered with its login, as a hidden field (a KDBX custom data item named PSSS_PASSWORD_RULES while the store is KDBX). Forget removes it.
    Rejected: A visible custom field (other apps would show it and a person might edit it); a global list of sites (a second place that leaks where the person has accounts).
    Why: It is the site’s rule, not a secret, and it makes “change password” work first time. It stays with the item when the item moves between vaults or is exported.
  11. In AutoFill, Use This Password unlocks, saves the login, and only then returns the password to the page. Still open.
    Rejected: Returning it first and saving on the later submit (a closed page loses the password); asking for a title or a vault; a confirmation after the fill.
    Why: A generated password the person never read must never be the only copy. The header’s own guidance for the filled event is to save as pending and ask nothing. The cost is a stray login if the sign-up is abandoned; it can be deleted with Undo.
  12. The recovery is a button, The Site Didn’t Accept It, with three answers in the words sites use. Still open.
    Rejected: One “Make it simpler” button stepping through presets (covers about two thirds of the awkward sites and never explains); sending the person to Customize (an expedition, and most cannot name the rule).
    Why: The person has the site’s error in front of them and can match it to one of three phrases. Each answer adds one rule to the same engine the request’s rules go through, and the rule is remembered with the login once it is saved.
  13. The no-request fallback is the editor card with a labeled Copy Password that saves a draft first.
    Rejected: A promise of save-after-submit on Mac (no API); a floating generator window; copying silently.
    Why: Honest about what Psss cannot see. The draft means switching apps to paste does not lose the login.
  14. Setup gets one row, Password Generator, which opens the same card with an example.
    Rejected: A settings screen of its own with separate controls (a second interface to keep in step); no row (nowhere to see what the default is).
    Why: One control, one place to learn it, and the row shows the current value. It adds one row to Setup, which stays under ten.
  15. No history of unused passwords, no pending marker, no usernames or aliases.
    Rejected: Bitwarden-style generator history.
    Why: A history would be a plaintext store of secrets the person never kept. The saved login’s Previous passwords is the only history.

Conflicts with Apple’s guidance and with project rules

  1. Two filled buttons on the Mac sheet (Use This Password inside it, Save below).
    Apple’s guidance is one default button per sheet. The card is a control inside the form, not the sheet’s confirm action, and the project rule is a visible, labeled action. The alternative is a bordered Use This Password on Mac only, a cheap change if the owner prefers it.
  2. A monospaced face at the title size for the password.
    Allowed by the project’s two-size rule (a face, not a third size). The trade-off is a face the rest of the screen does not use, which is the point: the password is an object.
  3. Bold digits and symbols instead of colour.
    An accessibility choice, not a departure from Apple. Some managers colour them; colour alone fails for some people and red is reserved here for deletion.
  4. Segmented controls for counts of four values.
    I believe this fits Apple’s guidance on a few exclusive choices, and the slider for a wide range, but I could not load the guideline pages to quote them. If the owner prefers steppers (Apple’s control for small increments), the cost is more taps and less visibility.
  5. A labeled button in the content instead of a toolbar symbol.
    Apple would put Generate in the top trailing position of the sheet. The project rule is visible, labeled actions. The cost is a taller sheet.
  6. On Mac the popup is Psss’s own.
    macOS has no system generate or save prompt for a provider (confirmed in the SDK headers). The extension’s popup is the only route in Safari; other browsers and apps get the editor card.
  7. The extension sheet follows the header’s rules.
    Cancel is always visible, as the header requires, and no vault data is read before Face ID.
  8. No exception to the secrets rule any more.
    The earlier draft showed the password by default. The masked preview removes the exception.

What it needs from the code

  • A new generator in App/Core/PasswordGenerator.swift with an injected random generator; an Apple-style builder (syllables, five digit positions, one capital, an offensive-substring filter of our own); a parser for Apple’s Password Rules language; a generated word list used only by Words; a settings value in the shared defaults with Apple-style as the default. Today’s generator is not Apple’s construction, ignores rules and can fail to include the characters it promises.
  • The provider reads passwordFieldPasswordRules, confirmPasswordFieldPasswordRules and passwordRulesFromQuirks, returns up to three results with Apple-style as strong, implements prepareInterface(for:) for generate (or sets SupportsGeneratePasswordCredentialsWithUI to false until it does), and handles the save event generatedPasswordFilled without a question.
  • A pure function for the pending-login matching so the event table can be tested without a host.
  • One SwiftUI card with the password field, masked or shown, used by the editor, the sheet, Setup and the Mac extension’s popup; a hidden passwordRules field on a login.
  • Platform facts not yet verified on a device: what the system shows when several results come back; how a person starts the user-initiated sheet; what a native app’s request carries; whether Safari turns length attributes into rules; what happens to a filled-but-unsubmitted form when the page closes; whether a pasteboard write works from the extension; and whether the VoiceOver slider moves one character at a time.
  • Verified from the SDK headers: the three generated-password kinds exist from iOS 26.2 and the kind is only a label, so Apple-style can be returned as strong. Verified in two sources, not Apple’s own page: the 20-character shape, the 19 consonants and 6 vowels, the five digit positions and the 71 bits. Not published, so reconstructed: the consonants, the capital positions and the offensive-word list.

Deliberately not built

  • A history of generated passwords.
  • A marker or reminder for a login saved before the site accepted it.
  • Usernames and email aliases.
  • Pronounceable passwords other than Apple-style, custom symbol sets, a choice of word list or language.
  • Strength checks on passwords the person types.
  • Settings that travel with the vault (section 6, if the owner prefers it).
  • A save-after-submit capture on Mac.
  • A visible first group on the masked password.
